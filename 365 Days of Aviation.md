Aviation 365 — understanding the complete aviation ecosystem
The central idea is to build a year-long course that teaches aviation as a connected operational, technical, regulatory and human system. Not simply 365 facts about aircraft, and not a pilot-training syllabus, but a structured journey from the international rules that govern aviation to the people, organisations, systems and physical processes that make a flight possible.

A student should eventually be able to follow a flight from the first commercial decision to operate it, through regulatory compliance, flight planning, aircraft preparation, passenger and baggage handling, air traffic management, departure, cruise, arrival and turnaround — and explain how safety, security, environmental performance, economics and technology affect every stage.

The course should follow the same principles as PracticAI 365:
- One focused lesson per day, designed for approximately 20–30 minutes.
- Seven-day learning cycles, moving from understanding to analysis, construction, challenge, application, integration and consolidation.
- Progressive maturity: each week assumes the knowledge developed in earlier weeks.
- Practical artefacts: diagrams, process maps, regulatory traceability tables, operational scenarios, calculations, data models and small projects.
- Cumulative integration: monthly projects connect previously separate subjects.
- Evidence over assertion: distinguish what a regulation requires, what an organisation chooses, what industry commonly does and what is merely proposed.
- A complete-system perspective: every subject is related to its upstream dependencies, downstream consequences and interfaces with other parts of aviation.
The organising principle is important: aviation is not a collection of independent disciplines. It is a system of systems. A flight can be technically airworthy yet unable to depart because of weather, crew limitations, an unavailable stand, a baggage problem, a failed communication service or an airspace restriction. The course should make those connections visible.
1. The architecture of Aviation 365
The curriculum will use a global foundation with a European operational emphasis. ICAO provides the international framework of Standards and Recommended Practices (SARPs), PANS and global plans; the EU establishes applicable legislation; EASA develops technical rules and guidance within its remit; national authorities implement and oversee their responsibilities; and individual organisations translate applicable requirements into operating procedures. These layers interact, but they are not interchangeable.

- EASA: the applicable rules and their associated Acceptable Means of Compliance (AMC), Guidance Material (GM), certification specifications and Easy Access Rules.
- National and organisational layers: national law, the competent authority, State safety programmes, licences, certificates, approvals, manuals and local procedures.
The EU regulatory map spans airworthiness, air operations, aircrew, aerodromes, ATM/ANS, airspace usage, rules of the air, UAS, information security and other areas. Ground handling also needs dedicated treatment rather than being treated as an afterthought.

The seven-day learning cycle

Day 1 — Understand

Establish the concept, purpose, terminology and place in the aviation system.



Day 2 — Derive

Trace dependencies, rules, responsibilities, interfaces and consequences.



Day 3 — Build

Create an artefact: a diagram, process, calculation, checklist, data model or structured analysis.



Day 4 — Challenge

Test assumptions, examine failures, identify hazards and find the limits of the model.



Day 5 — Apply

Use the knowledge in a realistic operational, regulatory or engineering scenario.



Day 6 — Integrate

Connect the week's subject to other aviation domains.



Day 7 — Consolidate

Demonstrate understanding, correct misconceptions and capture reusable learning.



This cadence applies throughout the curriculum. The topics below identify the principal learning objective for each day; the weekly exercises should progressively turn those objectives into a connected body of work.

4. How to teach the regulatory framework properly
One of the biggest risks with a course of this type is teaching document numbers without teaching how to use them.

The student should learn to distinguish:
- Requirement: what the applicable legal or regulatory text requires.
- Guidance: how compliance may be demonstrated or how a requirement may be interpreted.
- Certification or technical specification: the applicable technical criteria within its scope.
- Operational procedure: how a particular organisation implements its responsibilities.
- Industry practice: what organisations commonly do, which may or may not be mandatory.
- Proposal or future concept: something under development that must not be presented as an existing requirement.
For example, Regulation (EU) 2018/1139 establishes the EU's common civil aviation safety framework, while the relevant implementing rules provide more detailed requirements across domains. The applicable consolidated version must be checked when teaching or applying a specific provision.

5. The rules for the finished course
To keep Aviation 365 manageable and technically sound, I would adopt the following standards from the beginning.
1. One lesson, one primary learning outcome. A lesson may introduce connected concepts, but it should not attempt to teach an entire regulatory domain in half an hour.
2. Use real sources, but avoid reproducing whole rulebooks. Link to the official source and teach the student how to navigate it.
3. Separate general aviation knowledge from jurisdiction-specific requirements. The course's European emphasis must not be mistaken for a universal global rule.
4. Use scenarios with explicit assumptions. A fictional scenario is useful for learning, but must not be confused with operational instruction.
5. Revisit safety throughout the year. Safety management should not be confined to Month 10; each operational and technical topic should identify its relevant hazards and controls.
6. Teach interfaces, not just organisations. Every major topic should ask what information it receives, what service or decision it produces, and who depends on it.
7. Measure competence through artefacts. A good answer should show the reasoning, not merely repeat terminology.
8. Maintain a dated regulatory source register. Rules change, and a lesson should record the source version used when it was prepared.
The intended outcome is a student who can move confidently between the aircraft, airport, airline, ANSP, regulator and wider network — without assuming that expertise in one automatically confers expertise in another.
